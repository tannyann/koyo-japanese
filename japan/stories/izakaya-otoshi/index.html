Story 01 · The small dish you didn't order

Why do izakaya charge for a dish you didn't order?

Updated 22 Nov 20263 min read6 cards4 sources

By Sensei Japan — a local guide, born and raised in Japan, explaining the “why” behind everyday customs.

Short answer

That small dish is called otōshi (お通し) — tsukidashi in the Kansai region. At many izakaya and bars it arrives with your first drink and appears on the bill: it works like a cover charge for your seat, served as a small dish. It's a normal custom, not a mistake — and if the cost matters to you, ask before you sit down.

The story in 6 cards

What you notice, why, and your move

  1. 1/6Izakaya

    The small dish you didn't order

    What it is, why it's on your bill, and what to say.

    You'll meet it on your first night out in Japan. Here is the whole story in six cards — the same three beats as every story on this site: what you notice, why it happens, and what to do.

  2. 2/6You notice

    見

    A drink arrives — with a tiny dish you didn't order.

    It usually comes with your first drink, before anything you ordered — and later it's on the bill. Because most countries have no such custom, a Japanese tourism manual for restaurants notes that it can lead to complaints at the register.[2]

  3. 3/6The name

    名

    It's called otōshi.お通し · 関西では「突き出し」

    Japanese encyclopedias describe otōshi as a small serving that comes first and goes well with drinks. Otōshi is the Kanto (Tokyo-area) name; in Kansai (Osaka, Kyoto) it is called tsukidashi.[1]

  4. 4/6The reason

    理

    A bite for your first drink — and a charge for your seat.

    Traditionally it filled the wait until the food came: a small plate of something to go with drinks.[1] Today, the restaurant guide Gurunavi describes it as a kind of indirect cover charge at places that serve alcohol.[4]

  5. 5/6Where

    所

    Izakaya and bars: yes. Fast food and set meals: usually not.

    It's common at izakaya and other places that serve alcohol; fast food and set-price meals typically don't have it. What you get often changes by the day and may not be on the menu.[4]

  6. 6/6Your move

    動

    Ask before you sit: “Otōshi wa arimasu ka?”お通しはありますか？

    If the cost matters to you, ask at the start — not at the register. Tourism guidance in Japan encourages restaurants to explain otōshi to visitors, so asking is completely normal.[2][3] Then enjoy it.

At the table

Do this, skip that

Do

  • Ask before you sit if budget matters: “Otōshi wa arimasu ka?”
  • Give it a try. It often changes daily and may be something you'd never think to order.
  • Mention allergies when it arrives, so staff can tell you what's in it.

Avoid

  • Assuming it's free because you didn't order it.
  • Raising it for the first time at the register. Ask at the start instead.
  • Treating it as a trick. It's an everyday custom, not something aimed at tourists.

Questions

What people ask about otōshi

What is otōshi?

+

Otōshi is a small dish served at the start of a meal at many izakaya and bars, usually with your first drink and before the food you ordered. Otōshi is the Kanto (Tokyo-area) name; in the Kansai region (Osaka, Kyoto) it is called tsukidashi.

Why is otōshi on my bill?

+

At many izakaya and bars it works like a cover charge for your seat, served as a small dish. It is a normal custom in Japan, not a mistake on the bill.

Do all restaurants in Japan serve otōshi?

+

No. It is common at izakaya and other places that serve alcohol. Fast-food places and restaurants with set-price meals typically don't have it.

Can I say no to otōshi?

+

It depends on the place. If you would rather not have it, ask when you sit down, not at the register at the end. If staff explained the charge before you were seated, treat it as part of the price of the seat.

How do I ask about otōshi in Japanese?

+

Say “Otōshi wa arimasu ka?” (Is there an otōshi?) or “Otōshi-dai wa ikura desu ka?” (How much is the otōshi?).
